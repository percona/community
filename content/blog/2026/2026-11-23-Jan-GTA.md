November 19, 2026, was a pretty unusual day for software releases.

Two highly anticipated products finally reached general availability. Both had been delayed, both had people eagerly awaiting their arrival, and both have passionate communities that have followed their development.

One was [Grand Theft Auto VI](https://www.rockstargames.com/newswire/article/39akk424a9oo53/grand-theft-auto-vi-now-available). The other was [PostgreSQL 19](https://www.postgresql.org/about/news/postgresql-19-released-3449/).

Now, I realize that the overlap between people eagerly waiting for a new PostgreSQL release and those taking a day off to play GTA might not be huge. But I happen to be in that intersection.

## Two great products, worth the wait

Let's start with the obvious. Both GTA and PostgreSQL are fantastic products, even if they serve slightly different purposes. One lets you steal cars, while the other makes sure your transactions are ACID-compliant.

<div style="width:75%; margin: auto;">

![PG and GTA have some commonalities](blog/2026/11/Jan-PG-GTA-first.png)

</div>

But there are similarities:

- **Both were delayed.** GTA VI arrived 13 years after GTA V, following several postponements. [Rockstar explained the delay](https://www.rockstargames.com/newswire/article/ak3ak31a49a221/grand-theft-auto-vi-is-now-set-to-launch-november-19-2026) as necessary to deliver the expected level of polish. PostgreSQL 19 also missed its usual September/October release window as the community worked to stabilize the release, address bugs, and remove features that weren't ready. This became known as the "Vulnapocalypse," and [Zsolt has covered how LLM-assisted reviews impacted this release](https://percona.community/blog/2026/10/15/apologies-for-delaying-postgres-19/).
- **Both represent an enormous amount of work.** GTA VI's development budget has been rumored to exceed $1.5 billion. Some estimates even surpassed the [$1.5 billion it cost to construct the Burj Khalifa](https://www.ice.org.uk/what-is-civil-engineering/infrastructure-projects/burj-khalifa), though Rockstar hasn't yet confirmed these numbers. PostgreSQL, meanwhile, is the result of [over three decades of open source development](https://www.postgresql.org/about/press/faq/), thousands of contributions, and a global community working in the open.

Then came the memes about people calling in sick, taking holidays, or disappearing from work for days. It wasn't just memes either. Don't get me wrong. I'm also hyped, and I certainly don't think proprietary software is inherently bad. But something about this launch bothered me. The End User License Agreement (EULA) and the privacy terms that come with it.

With all this excitement, how many people actually read what they accepted before playing?

A quick survey among a few friends who installed GTA VI on launch day revealed that none had read the terms. They'd already paid for the game, so they just wanted to get through the agreement and start playing.

It's nothing new. [South Park already made fun of people not reading Apple's user agreement](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/blog/2011/apr/29/apple-tracking-south-park) more than 15 years ago in the "HUMANCENTiPAD" episode. Seems like the absurd comedy from 2011 is even more relevant today…

## What exactly are we agreeing to?

<div style="width:75%; margin: auto;">

![Reading the license before hitting Accept](blog/2026/11/Jan-PG-GTA-license.png)

</div>

I read through the [Rockstar's privacy policy](https://www.rockstargames.com/privacy), updated in March 2026. Let's say parts of it raised my eyebrows.

In "Internet / Electronic Activity," Rockstar lists collecting web and app browsing information associated with its services, gameplay information, details about the games and platforms used, and other information relating to installed applications.

It also describes collecting hardware and software details, device identifiers, and information used to build personalized profiles for advertising.

I understand why a game might need information about your hardware, gameplay, or interactions with online services. But why does a game publisher need information about other installed applications? Which applications? What information about them? And for what purpose?

The policy is broad enough that I can't answer these questions. As a privacy concerned person it actually prevents me from playing.

To be clear, this doesn't prove that GTA VI scans every installed application or monitors everything you do. [It launched on consoles only](https://www.rockstargames.com/newswire/article/39akk424a9oo53/grand-theft-auto-vi-now-available), where applications are generally more isolated than on PCs. The policy also covers Rockstar's services generally, not just GTA VI. 

Still, that's quite a broad scope for something most people think of as simply playing a game.

And then there's data retention.

[Rockstar's policy](https://www.rockstargames.com/privacy) says it retains personal information for as long as necessary to provide services, fulfill legal obligations, or defend its rights. It may also retain certain information after an account becomes inactive.

So how long, exactly? That's not particularly clear to me as a potential user and sure does read like  “however long we like”. 

## Can I just say no and keep playing?

Rockstar's [Terms of Service](https://www.rockstargames.com/legal) explicitly state that if you don't agree to the terms, you're not permitted to use its services.

But accepting contractual terms and agreeing to every possible use of your personal information are two different things.

Rockstar does offer [privacy controls and advertising opt-outs](https://www.rockstargames.com/ccpa). Its policy also recognizes the right to withdraw consent where consent is the legal basis for processing.

Some collection is justified as necessary to provide services, particularly online functionality. Other processing relies on legal obligations or legitimate interests, while some activities rely on consent.

But I couldn't find in the published documentation what happens if I only want to play the story?

[PlayStation confirms that GTA VI supports offline single-player gameplay](https://www.playstation.com/en-us/games/grand-theft-auto-vi/). Can I refuse nonessential data collection and still enjoy the game I've purchased? Does Rockstar collect information about other installed applications during offline play, or is that relevant only to particular services?

I couldn't find a clear, GTA VI-specific answer.

I cannot say a way for a user to do so exists and I’m tempted enough to buy the game just to try it.

## But isn't Europe protected by GDPR?

<div style="width:75%; margin: auto;">

![GDPR billboards over Vice City](blog/2026/11/Jan-PG-GTA-GDPR.png)

</div>

This is where I got confused. I live in Europe and I thought we'd already sorted out some of these issues.

Anyone who's gone through corporate GDPR training has probably heard about [purpose limitation, data minimization, transparency, and storage limitation](https://commission.europa.eu/law/law-topic/data-protection/information-business-and-organisations/principles-gdpr_en).

My understanding is simple: collect what you need, explain why you need it, and don't keep it longer than necessary.

A broadly worded privacy policy doesn't guarantee a GDPR violation. There is no universal maximum retention period, and not all processing requires consent.

But European data-protection authorities define that for consent to be valid, it must meet four cumulative criteria and be: [freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous](https://www.edpb.europa.eu/our-work-tools/our-documents/guidelines/guidelines-052020-consent-under-regulation-2016679_en). So bundling optional processing into a mandatory agreement doesn't make it valid consent.

What information is necessary to play GTA VI, particularly offline? What collection is optional? Can I refuse nonessential processing without losing access to a product I've already purchased?

Rockstar does provide a [way to request access to personal information](https://www.take2games.com/data-request), as required under GDPR. That's good. But shouldn't I be able to understand what's being collected without having to file a data-access request?

Perhaps Rockstar has perfectly reasonable answers. I'd would very much like to hear them. My hope is that more people will be concerned and ask these loud enough for us to get the answers. For now surprisingly there is the no real discussion about this. The launch hype focused around graphics, performance, delays, and whether the game was worth the wait. But not much talk around terms under which we're allowed to enjoy the game.

Did we over the years simply got used to clicking Accept?

## And this is where PostgreSQL shines

PostgreSQL takes a radically different approach.

It is distributed under the [PostgreSQL License](https://www.postgresql.org/about/licence/), a permissive open source license that is refreshingly short (not like this blog post is shaping up to be…).

You can use it for virtually any purpose, study it, modify it, redistribute it, and build products around it. You don't need permission from the PostgreSQL Global Development Group to use it commercially, and you don't have to agree to send telemetry to its developers.

You can even build a competing database product using PostgreSQL! Plenty of companies do, contributing to a growing ecosystem.

Being open source doesn't automatically guarantee privacy. But it gives users something particularly valuable: control.

The source code is [available for inspection](https://git.postgresql.org/gitweb/?p=postgresql.git), development happens [in public](https://www.postgresql.org/list/pgsql-hackers/), and you can decide where and how to run the software. You can operate PostgreSQL entirely on infrastructure you control, without sending usage information to a vendor.

And you can walk away from a commercial provider without walking away from the database itself.

That's what software freedom means to me. Not everything being free of charge, but not having to surrender control simply to use excellent software.

## Happy to support PostgreSQL 19 from day one

November 19 was also special for another reason.

<div style="width:75%; margin: auto;">

![PostgreSQL 19 Major Release Edition game box from Percona](blog/2026/11/Jan-PG-GTA-Percona.png)

</div>

Just hours after the PostgreSQL Global Development Group announced PostgreSQL 19, Percona released Percona Distribution for PostgreSQL 19.0.1.

Releasing PostgreSQL packages on the same day as upstream is already an achievement. Doing it with a complete distribution is another beast.

We're talking about PostgreSQL together with the [open source tools enterprises need](https://docs.percona.com/percona-for-postgresql/) for high availability, backups, monitoring, security, and day-to-day operations.

Including two capabilities especially close to my heart, and particularly important in regulated environments:

- [Transparent Data Encryption (TDE) with pg_tde](https://docs.percona.com/pg-tde/index.html)
- [OpenID Connect (OIDC) authentication with pg_oidc_validator](https://www.percona.com/blog/postgresql-oidc-authentication-with-pg_oidc_validator/)

Both represent something I deeply believe in: sophisticated enterprise capabilities shouldn't require surrendering the open source values.

Getting all these components ready for a major PostgreSQL release takes far more than recompiling a database server. Just a year ago, delivering our PostgreSQL 18 distribution took roughly two months after upstream. This time, it took hours.

I'm hugely grateful to our developers, QA, and packaging engineers. Thank you for showing what a focused and determined team can accomplish!

## Two releases, two philosophies

I'll probably enjoy GTA VI as much as everyone else. And I have enormous respect for the people who spent years building it. Great software deserves recognition regardless of its licensing model.

But November 19 reminded me that there's something worth celebrating beyond the software itself.

One product comes with lengthy conditions governing how you can use it. The other gives you the freedom to build your own.

And while the streets of Vice City may be more exciting than a PostgreSQL instance on most occasions, every experienced DBA knows that databases aren't always (unfortunatelly) boring either.

Happy release day to both communities.

And congratulations to everyone who made PostgreSQL 19 happen.
