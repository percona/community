Autumn is the time when leaves change color and fall. It's also the time when new PostgreSQL releases come out, or at least that's been the tradition for quite a while.

There's one more thing that autumn brings to the PostgreSQL world: [PGConf.EU](https://pgconf.eu/), the annual conference that has grown into the largest PostgreSQL community event.

This year we met in Valencia, Spain. And while I've attended several editions already, 2026 brought quite a few firsts for me.

## 2026 brought a lot of firsts
<img src="/blog/2026/11/jan-pgc-one.png" alt="Elephants celebrating their PGConf.EU 2026 firsts" style="display:block;margin:1rem auto;width:50%;border-radius:6px;">

### 1. A proper conference venue! 
For the first time, I attended a [PGConf.EU 2026](https://2026.pgconf.eu/) that wasn't hosted in a hotel. And what an improvement!

The acoustics at Valencia's conference center were better than anything I've experienced at the previous three editions. I still remember (and dread) the noise levels in Athens.

Kudos to the organizing team for making this brave decision. Moving away from the familiar hotel format was clearly a challenge, but I don't think the conference atmosphere or networking suffered at all. Quite the opposite. Having more space made the whole experience more comfortable without losing the community feeling. 

### 2. Talking about a PostgreSQL release that hadn't quite arrived

This was also the first PGConf.EU 2026 I attended where a new PostgreSQL major release was still waiting for its final release date. If my search results are correct then the last time it happened was over a decade ago when Vienna PGConf.EU in 2025 happened before the release of PostgreSQL 9.5 later in January of 2016. So PGConf.EU 2026 was the first occurrence in 11 years, breaking a streak that started in 2016.

PostgreSQL 19 had reached RC1 just before the conference, but its general availability had moved to November. That created an interesting situation for speakers presenting new PostgreSQL 19 features.

At this stage, we can be fairly confident that what's in the release candidate will make it into the final version. But fairly confident isn't the same as certain. I can only imagine how stressful that must have been for some speakers.
The ongoing "vulnapocalypse" has certainly taken its toll on the project.

One positive thing that many of us kept coming back to during conversations was that the pressure wasn't simply the result of LLM-generated slop flooding the project. Gen AI-assisted tools have also helped uncover real security issues that needed attention.

Hopefully, things will become more manageable as we learn to incorporate these tools into our everyday workflows. I suspect the current pace of discoveries won't continue forever, but we'll need better processes for handling them either way.

For now, it's reassuring to see that the project is willing to delay a release rather than compromise on quality and security. It’s also great to see engagement and overall understanding coming from the broad community in the face of such a crisis. I have not heard one person “whining” it was more of a motivated discussion with everyone eager to work towards a better future.

### 3. Presenting with someone I'd never met in person

For the first time, I gave a conference talk with a speaker from another company. More importantly, someone I'd never met in person before the event.

I have to admit, this was a little stressful.

David Steele and I had worked remotely on the presentation and I felt very positive about what we'd prepared. But there's a difference between working together online and standing on stage together in front of an audience.

Thankfully, David turned out to be an amazingly kind and thoughtful person in real life. We clicked immediately and I genuinely enjoyed presenting with him. 

All's well that ends well. And this one ended very well!

### 4. Seeing the Call for Papers process from the other side

This was my first time serving on a Call for Papers (CfP) committee.

The experience deserves its own blog post and I certainly intend to write one. For now, I'll just say that it taught me a lot.

Reviewing submissions gives you a completely different perspective on what makes a good conference proposal. It also forces you to reflect on your own submissions and how you can make them clearer, more compelling and easier for others to evaluate. Lastly it leaves you with a lot of appreciation for the otherwise invisible hard work of the reviewing committee. Kudos to all the CfP committees of all the past conferences I’ve attended cannot be emphasized enough!

While very time consuming, I hope I'll have the opportunity to do it again.

### 5. Experiencing Community Day

Another first for me was participating in the Community Events Day following the main conference. Not before as it used to be before. 

Nothing else to say than that I loved it!

The discussions were productive and, hopefully, will lead to some great new initiatives (new mailing list being one of them grinning face with smiling eyes). But what surprised me most was how much I appreciated the smaller crowds.
While I love the main PGConf.EU event for how big it is, with fewer people around, it was easier to have longer conversations, meet fellow attendees and discuss ideas without constantly rushing to the next session. Also longer sessions with a more of an unconference vibes gave more space for organized discussions.
Here I have to thank the teams behind the sessions to which I attended Floor Drees and Alastair Turner as well as Chris Ellis, Valeria Kaplan and Loreta Ozolina. These guys did an amazing job to keep us in check and drive the discussions towards results that I hope will make PostgreSQL Community a better one! I really hope this format continues to grow and we’ll meet on a next Community Day after PGConf.EU 2027. 

## Community is strong in this one
<img src="/blog/2026/11/jan-pgc-this.png" alt="Elephants learning from a wise elephant teacher in a swamp" style="display:block;margin:1rem auto;width:50%;border-radius:6px;">

Some things haven't changed at all.

[PGConf.EU 2026](https://2026.pgconf.eu/) remains one of the best places to experience the strength of the PostgreSQL Community. 

To really understand why this is a community conference, rather than just a conference about PostgreSQL, you have to venture beyond the official schedule. Unfortunately not everyone knows this, for me it took some time to get the most of these events.

To give you some examples how to get more out of the future events I would start off by joining all the Conference specific Telegram channels that are communicated by the organizers before the event. Next steps require more engagement, to give you some examples of what can help experience the Community vibe in full this can be:

Going for a morning run with fellow attendees

Joining a pub crawl or going out for a meal

Singing (or bravely attempting to sing) at community karaoke

Exploring the city together, also after the event as a lot of attendees stay for the following weekend

Organizing an excursion and inviting others along

Of course, none of these activities is unique to PostgreSQL conferences. The difference is who you do them with.

At many industry events, social activities revolve around colleagues, customers or business partners. At PGConf.EU 2026, you might spend the evening with people you met during the hallway track, people from competing companies or people you've known for years but only ever see at PostgreSQL events. To be honest while we work in competing companies, we find ourselves building relationships after hours with fellow PostgreSQL engineers from across the industry.

You start with a technical discussion and end up bonding over something that has absolutely nothing to do with databases.

It may seem like a small thing, but it makes a huge difference.

People genuinely make an effort to include others, extend invitations and make newcomers feel welcome. It's not just about meeting the people you already know.

I realize that not everyone has the energy or inclination to socialize with strangers. And there's nothing wrong with that. But for me, this is where much of the value of PGConf.EU 2026  lies.

It's also probably why these conferences leave me so physically and emotionally exhausted.

The good kind of exhausted. Mostly.

## No better place to get the PostgreSQL team together
<img src="/blog/2026/11/jan-pgc-team.png" alt="Elephants having fun in a giant paella" style="display:block;margin:1rem auto;width:50%;border-radius:6px;">

For the second year in a row, Percona PostgreSQL Engineering used PGConf.EU 2026 as an opportunity to bring the team together ahead of the event. This time, our focus was planning for 2027.

From a practical perspective, it makes a lot of sense.

Many of our engineers attend PostgreSQL conferences anyway. Bringing a few additional colleagues to the same location is considerably easier and less expensive than arranging a completely separate team gathering.

But the real value goes beyond logistics.

As a remote-first company, we don't get many opportunities to spend time together in person. And those opportunities matter.

Face-to-face conversations help build personal connections and trust that carry over into our everyday remote work.

After all, we've probably all experienced how emotionally detached a Slack message can feel. Sometimes a short message reads very differently once you actually know the person behind it.

A few days together can make months of remote collaboration easier.

## No better track than a hallway track
<img src="/blog/2026/11/jan-pgc-hallway.png" alt="PostgreSQL elephant walking the red carpet of the hallway track" style="display:block;margin:1rem auto;width:50%;border-radius:6px;">
 

Most conference sessions are recorded. Unfortunately, some of the most valuable ones aren't.

I'm talking, of course, about the hallway track.

This probably deserves to be one of the first highlights of my recap. Then again, it's not something unique to PGConf.EU 2026. In my experience, it's one of the greatest benefits of attending conferences in general. One that some newcomers unfortunately miss out a lot. 

Especially now that so many of us work remotely this grows so much in importance.

There's something special about being surrounded by fellow nerds and having spontaneous conversations about anything and everything, work-related or otherwise.

I get tremendous pleasure from catching up with people I only meet at events like this. Some of them I've now met at four editions of PGConf.EU 2026, without seeing them once in between.

And yet, when we meet again, the conversation picks up almost exactly where we left it. Sometimes these discussions end up with hiring, some of them end up with companies becoming our customers. That was never the intention, the intention is the spontaneous discussion. 

There's also a PostgreSQL specific practical side to this.

It's interesting how sharing the same physical space can help move ideas forward that have struggled to gain traction remotely. 

Sometimes you've been advocating for something for months, exchanging messages, arranging calls and trying to get the right people involved. Then a fifteen-minute conversation in a conference hallway finally makes things click.

No formal agenda. No calendar invitation. Just the right people talking at the right moment.

That's difficult to reproduce online.

## What’s there in the future?
<img src="/blog/2026/11/jan-pgc-future.png" alt="Elephant fortune tellers looking into a crystal ball" style="display:block;margin:1rem auto;width:50%;border-radius:6px;">
 
As this event has closed I do have three wishes for the next one:

### 1. Let's break the attendance record

Athens in 2024 still holds the record for the largest PGConf.EU 2026 .

I'd love to see next year's conference beat that record and bring us back to a trend of annual growth.

Not because bigger is always better, but because I'd love to see more people experience what makes this community special. 

### 2. Let's make something happen after Community Day

The PostgreSQL Ecosystem Health and Community Outreach sessions generated some interesting discussions and ideas.

But good discussions are only the beginning.

We've only just returned home, so it's too early to judge their impact. I hope we'll keep the momentum going and turn at least some of those conversations into real initiatives.

My wish is that next year we'll meet again, not to restart the same discussions, but to review what we've accomplished and decide what comes next.

That would be a great measure of success for Community Day.

### 3. Let's organize a PostgreSQL Beer Hike

Radim Marek and I discussed the idea of an unofficial PostgreSQL Beer Hike. A great example of hallway track that’s not necessarily business related.
As it turns out we both love beer and mountain hiking. In any combination slightly smiling face A simple idea was born: get people together, go for a hike, enjoy some beer and spend time with fellow community members outside the usual conference environment.

I hope that by next year's PGConf.EU 2026 we'll be able to say that we actually made it happen.

And perhaps even give a lightning talk about it, encouraging others to organize similar activities.

After all, building a strong community doesn't always require another conference session.

Sometimes all you need is a trail, a few people and a good beer.
